When I heard the news that hajj registration might become a ticket war, I kind of agreed with part of it. The official wait is about 26 years and every new registration makes it longer, so something has to stop that.

But a plain ticket war would be cruel to the people who already registered, especially the ones who have waited the longest. That's why the stage rules in part 1 treat people differently depending on how close they are to departure.

This part lists the policy choices behind those rules. For each one, I list what it costs and what the other option would cost.

## Closing the current registration

The choice: nobody new can join the Rp 25 million queue.

What it costs: a person who wants to go has no way in except the war for new registrants, and that war may never open. More on that below.

The other option is to keep both. The benefit is that anyone can still join and wait their turn.

The cost is that the queue keeps growing, and the official wait of about 26 years keeps getting longer.

## Starting the war right away

The choice: the new rules apply from the first season, so stage 3 is already a war then. There's no transition period.

What it costs: someone scheduled 5 years out can lose a seat to someone scheduled much further out who pays faster. I accept that because they still have years left, and anyone who keeps losing eventually moves into stage 2, where queue number decides. It's still a cost, and it lands on people who waited.

The other option is to delay the war and keep today's system for a while. The benefit is that everyone's queue number stays meaningful.

The cost is that the delay has no natural end. More than 200k people register every year, and there are about 203k seats, so the queue doesn't shrink on its own. The 26-year wait stays where it is for as long as we wait.

## First pay first served in stage 3

The choice: speed decides in stage 3, and queue number decides in stage 2.

What it costs: speed means connection and device. Someone with a fast phone in a city beats someone with a slow connection in a remote district, and the system can't fix that. The only thing it does is move the preparation, the money and the health check, to before the war opens.

It also repeats. Someone who loses the war has to be ready again next season, and I assume they pay for the health check again, because the supporting tests typically stay valid for only about 3 months. A person who keeps losing carries that cost every year, until their schedule moves them into stage 2.

The other option is queue number in every stage. The benefit is that it's fairer to the time people have waited.

The cost is time. The system would have to invite people one by one in queue order and wait for each of them to pay. Seats nobody pays for go to the next person in line. Stage 3 has millions of people for about a thousand seats, and many of them won't be ready to pay, so it would take wave after wave. Each wave needs days, and stage 3 only has about a week. All of that would protect people who still have years left.

## Not refunding existing registrants

The choice: deposits stay where they are.

What it costs: someone scheduled 30 years out has their money locked for a seat they may never see, and they can't leave. The reason I keep it is the returns on every waiting pilgrim's deposit. They cover about 44% of the hajj cost for 2027, which is Rp 43.8 million of Rp 98.5 million per pilgrim. Refunding everyone would shrink what funds the people who are about to depart.

The other option is to refund everyone, or everyone except those departing within 7 years. The benefit is that it's the fairest to people with a long wait, since they get their money back and can leave.

The cost is a funding hole, because that money is what produces the returns. Nothing like a mass refund has happened before, and how big the hole would be, I don't know. That's my reasoning and not a calculation.

## The war for new registrants may never happen

The choice: new registrants get seats only after earlier stages leave some. There's no fixed share for them.

What it costs: the war that gives the policy its name, stage 4, almost certainly never opens. Stage 3 has millions of people competing for about a thousand seats nationwide, so it sells out and nothing is left. The war that actually happens is stage 3, and it's among people who are already registered.

Stage 4 only opens if the old queue runs dry or stage 3 fails to sell out. With an official wait of about 26 years, that's decades away. So for a person who never registered, the policy is a closed door that is supposed to open someday.

The other option is a fixed share of the quota for new registrants, whatever size. The benefit is that the war for them happens every season, so they have a real chance at a seat.

The cost is that new registrants take seats from people who registered first. Those people get fewer seats, so they wait longer.

## A small tolerance for overbooking

The choice: stage 1 overbooks a little by also taking payments from reserves, limited to 0.5% of a province's quota. Only stage 1 can overbook, and only people scheduled for next season can be one. A reserve doesn't hold a seat. They only go if a seat opens because someone paid and then dropped out, and otherwise they roll into stage 0 next season, which is where they were headed anyway.

The limit is small on purpose. Every reserve who doesn't get a seat lands in next season's stage 0, and a big limit would stack it and leave less room for that season's stage 1.

In the 2026 season, reserve pilgrims who had paid pushed round 2 above 100% of the quota, and about 99.6% of the seats were filled in the end. That's one season, so it's a data point and not a pattern.

The other number is how many pilgrims pay and then fail to depart. A 2025 statement by the hajj ministry put it at 800 to 1.2k a year, about 0.5% of a 221k quota, and that's where the limit comes from.

What it costs: some reserves pay the remaining balance and don't get a seat this season, so their money is tied up for another year. Part 3 says the system can never take money for a seat it can't give, so reserves have to know they're reserves when they pay.

The other option is no tolerance at all, only as many payers as seats. The benefit is that nobody pays and then has to wait another year for a seat.

The cost is wasted seats. A seat that nobody takes at the last minute is lost, since Saudi Arabia's quota can't be saved for next year, and a pilgrim who drops out after the replacement window closes leaves an empty seat.
